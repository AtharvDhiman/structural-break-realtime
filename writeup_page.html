Watching for a break in real time

How I built a calibrated, debuggable break detector — and what the metric taught me before any modelling did.

Every number is on held-out series. The gap from 0.5 is small because the task is hard; the gap between rungs is where the reasoning lives.

You're handed a univariate time series in two parts: a long historical stretch with no break in it, then a shorter online stretch fed to you one point at a time. After every point you emit a number in [0,1] — your running confidence that a permanent break has already happened.

The breaks are a mixed bag: shifts in mean, in variance, in distributional shape, in dependence structure. Some series never break. Half of mine did. I was after a solid, debuggable submission and a clear story, not a leaderboard crown — so this is a working note, dead ends included.

The thing that decided everything else was the scoring metric, so that's where I'll start.

00 — The metric

The metric is the whole game

The score is a Time-Stratified AUC. At each online step you take the live series, label the ones that have already broken as positives, compute an ordinary cross-sectional AUC across series, and average those per-step AUCs with a weight w(t) = n_pos(t) · n_neg(t).

That weight is the same quantity sitting in the denominator of AUC at that step — the number of positive–negative pairs. So w(t)·AUC(t) is just the count of correctly-ordered pairs, and the whole thing collapses to one number: total correctly-ordered pairs across all steps, over total pairs. It's a single micro-averaged AUC where comparisons only ever happen between series at the same step.

Where the metric's weight actually sits

Half the total weight sits before step ~290. The first ten steps are worth almost nothing — which quietly killed the idea of predicting break-presence from history to beat 0.5 early.

And there's a second consequence that shaped the core of the solution. At scoring time you never see the other series — you walk one to its end before the next begins, and the platform assembles the cross-section afterward. Carrying state between series is technically allowed but breaks the determinism check. So each series has to be scored on an absolute, self-contained scale, and the only way a raw statistic from one series is comparable to another's is to express it relative to that series' own behaviour. Per-series calibration isn't a nice-to-have here. The interface forces it.

01 — Calibration

Raw statistics are the wrong output

Use a CUSUM straight and its size depends on the series' own noise and history length. A naturally volatile but unbroken series out-scores a quiet series that genuinely broke — and since the metric only cares about ranking across series, you'd be ranking by volatility, not by breaks.

So I convert every statistic into an empirical quantile under that series' own null. The historical segment is a free, break-free null sample. For a statistic on the first t online points, I compare it against the same statistic on every length-t window of the history, and emit where it falls. Using every contiguous window makes it deterministic and preserves the series' own dependence; matching the window length keeps the comparison fair as the online segment grows.

On the simplest statistic I had, raw scored 0.518 and calibrated moved it to 0.533. A small lift, but two things were instructive: two-sided calibration beat one-sided, which means a real fraction of breaks make series quieter; and the ceiling was clearly the statistic, not the calibration.

02 — Residuals

One framework for four break types

A single hand-picked statistic can't see all four break types, so I fit a model to the history and monitored its residuals. An AR captures the conditional mean, a GARCH the conditional variance, and the standardised residual z = e / √h should be white noise with unit variance while nothing has changed. I checked it held on unbroken series (mean ≈ 0, variance ≈ 1, autocorrelation ≈ 0) and that the recursion was leakage-free.

Then each break type shows up as a specific departure of z from white noise, and I built one calibrated statistic for each: level (running mean of z), variance (mean of z²), tail/shape (rate of |z|>2), dependence (running lag-1 autocorrelation).

Variance was the workhorse — already past the single calibrated statistic. The surprise was the level term: weak, and actively anti-informative late in the series. My best guess is GARCH: a mean jump inflates the residual, the model reads it as a burst of volatility and divides it back out, so the level shift hides in the variance channel instead. And a naive equal-weight average of the four scored below the single best one — blending diluted the strong signal with the weak. That's the argument for learning the combination rather than averaging.

03 — Stacking

The things that didn't work

I stacked the calibrated statistics with a gradient-boosted model. Three things I'd flagged as plausible turned out wrong — and I only know because I tested them instead of assuming.

A running max on the output, to keep confidence from falling, hurt. Under good calibration an unbroken series emits roughly uniform scores, and a running max ratchets those toward 1 as the series lengthens — poisoning the negative class at exactly the middle steps that carry the weight.

Feeding the running max in as a feature instead was strictly better. Adding the historical length as a feature overfit — the model latched onto a length↔break correlation that didn't survive to new series, and dropping it raised the score. And throwing a dozen extra statistics at the model overfit below the lean set: the original four worked precisely because they were non-redundant, one per break type. More wasn't better; better-curated was.

Not a leaderboard-topping number, but every step is something I can explain and debug. The submission is deterministic — no RNG, no cross-series state — and the streaming path is bit-identical to the offline pipeline.

The push past this number is a story about what didn't work, and it rhymes with the last section. Adding more statistics overfit. Selecting the AR order per series hurt — tighter whitening erased break signal along with the dependence. Weighting the training toward the metric's middle steps hurt. A Kolmogorov distributional statistic hurt. Every lever that added something cost me. The one change that helped went the other way: halving the number of trees, which trimmed a little overfitting and nudged it from 0.569 to 0.575. On this problem, the gains came from taking complexity away, not piling it on.

The one thing I'll keep

On a rank-based, cross-sectional metric, the modelling barely matters until you've made your scores comparable across series. Getting the calibration right was worth more than any single detector — and it was the one part the interface wouldn't let me skip.
