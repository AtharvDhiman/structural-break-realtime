Everything looks like noise — until it doesn't.

Picture a sensor on a factory machine, the beat of someone's heart, or the water level of a river. Read any of them often enough and you get a jittery stream of numbers. Nearly all of that jitter means nothing.

But now and then, something real happens. The machine starts to wear out. The patient's condition shifts. The river changes course. The numbers stop wiggling around their old normal and settle into a new one. Statisticians call that a structural break — a permanent change in how the data behaves.

The hard part isn't spotting it a year later, when it's obvious. It's spotting it now, while the readings are still coming in one at a time — and not crying wolf every time the noise gets a little loud.

HOW IT WORKSThree simple ideas

1

Learn what calm looks like

First it studies a long stretch of the stream from before anything changed — long enough to learn that particular stream's idea of normal, and especially how much it usually jumps around.

2

Judge every stream by its own past

As each new number arrives, it asks: is this still normal for this stream? A reading that's shocking for a calm stream might be routine for a wild one. So it always grades on a curve set by that stream's own history — never a one-size-fits-all threshold.

3

Let the evidence add up

One odd reading proves nothing — noise does that all the time. But when the surprises keep stacking up in the same direction, confidence grows and stays. A real break is permanent, so the evidence for it shouldn't fade.

THE HONEST TRUTHDoes it actually work?

Yes — but it's a nudge, not a crystal ball, and it's worth being honest about that. Across thousands of test streams, it ranks the ones that truly changed above the ones that didn't noticeably more often than a coin flip would. That sounds modest, and it is: catching a permanent change early, without false alarms, from a single noisy stream, is genuinely one of the harder things to do in statistics.

The real win here isn't a flashy number. It's a method you can actually follow, step by step, and check for yourself — which, for a tool making calls on live data, matters more than a little extra accuracy nobody can explain.
